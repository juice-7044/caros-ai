# Your Past Clients Are Moving Again. Will They Call You?

You recognize the kitchen before you recognize the address.

The new listing belongs to a client you helped buy three years ago. Another agent's sign is out front.

There's no dramatic breakup story. You did good work. Then both of you got busy.

Real estate past client follow up gives that relationship a place in your operating system after closing day.

It doesn't guarantee repeat business. People move, preferences change, and clients choose who serves them next.

But silence shouldn't be your default retention plan.

None of these are failures of good real estate pros. They're failures of process.

## Closing should change the workflow, not end it

During a transaction, every deadline has an owner. After closing, the relationship often becomes “remember to check in.”

That task competes with every active deal on your calendar.

Create a deliberate handoff from transaction to relationship care. Confirm the contact's preferred details, communication preferences, and whether occasional updates would be welcome.

Keep a named owner. Record the closing date and any useful follow-up the client actually requested.

Don't treat a past transaction as blanket permission for every future campaign. Use appropriate channels and follow your brokerage's approved outreach process.

The goal is a relationship the client recognizes, not a database entry you can repeatedly message.

## Make each touch useful enough to deserve attention

A generic home-anniversary message may be pleasant. Repeating the same message every year rarely creates a conversation.

Add a concrete offer of help.

“It's been a year since closing. Is there a homeownership question I can help you find the right person to answer?”

Or ask whether a concise local market update would be useful. Keep any numbers dated and sourced. Avoid implying you know the home's current value without doing the necessary work.

For homeowners who request planning help, offer a conversation about their goals. Don't assume a life event means they should sell.

Sphere contacts need the same respect. A former coworker is a relationship, not an automatic subscriber.

Relevance comes from what people tell you and what they agree to receive.

## Build a calendar your team can maintain

A complicated campaign that nobody reviews creates a different kind of silence: lots of output, little attention.

Start with a small calendar.

**After closing:** send a genuine thank-you and explain how to reach you with follow-up questions. Separate any review request from service follow-up so the client can respond comfortably.

**At an agreed early check-in:** ask whether anything remains unresolved. Route questions to the right professional rather than improvising answers.

**At an anniversary or requested milestone:** offer relevant help and confirm contact preferences.

**At the client's chosen frequency:** send useful updates with a clear identity, reason for contact, and easy way to stop.

**When a client replies:** pause the general campaign and hand the conversation to the owner.

This is a starting framework. A client who wants one annual email shouldn't receive monthly texts because your template says so.

## Reactivate old records with context, not a blast

Your database shouldn't be a digital graveyard. Still, waking every record at once can create a queue your team can't serve.

Start by reviewing data quality and contact permissions. Remove duplicates. Preserve opt-outs. Identify contacts already speaking with someone on your team.

Then choose a small group you can follow through with personally.

Use a message grounded in a real relationship:

“Hi [Name], it's [Agent] from [Brokerage]. We worked together on [appropriate context]. Would an occasional homeownership or market update still be useful? If not, no problem.”

Use that template only where the channel and permissions support it. If a record's history is unclear, resolve that before outreach.

Don't invent a reason you “were just thinking of them.” A clear, honest message is warmer than fabricated familiarity.

## Ask for referrals without making the relationship transactional

A satisfied past client may introduce you to someone. They don't owe you introductions.

Make the offer easy to understand and easy to decline.

“If someone you know needs help planning a move, I'm happy to be a resource. They can contact me directly, or you can introduce us if everyone is comfortable.”

That gives the client control. It also avoids collecting another person's details without context.

When an introduction happens, record who made it and thank them appropriately. Follow brokerage policy for any proposed gifts or incentives.

Keep referral appreciation separate from pressure to provide a positive public review. Ask for honest feedback and let the client choose.

## Show the economics of staying in touch

Repeat business still costs time and money. Calling it “free revenue” hides the work.

Consider an illustrative annual program for 200 past-client households. Assume content, tools, and staff time cost $2,400 in total.

That's **$12 per household per year**, calculated as $2,400 ÷ 200.

Suppose four repeat or referred transactions close during the same period, each producing an assumed $4,000 retained commission. That's **$16,000 before program and other operating costs**.

Subtracting the $2,400 program expense leaves $13,600 before remaining business costs. This is a hypothetical scenario, not a CAROS result or a promised return.

Attribution needs care. Those four transactions may reflect years of good service, personal relationships, recent outreach, or all three.

Ask clients how they returned or heard about you. Record the answer alongside system-tracked touches. Don't give a single anniversary email all the credit.

## Track relationship health before the next listing appears

A repeat-client closing is a useful outcome. It arrives too late to tell you whether this month's process worked.

Track a few earlier signals:

- Past-client records with a named owner and valid preferences.
- Promised follow-ups completed on time.
- Useful two-way conversations.
- Planning consultations requested and attended.
- Repeat and referred opportunities, with the relationship source preserved.

Also review bounced messages, opt-outs, and replies asking for less contact. A cleaner, smaller list can be more valuable than a large list nobody wants to hear from.

Compare cohorts over appropriate periods. Don't infer that every quiet household has been lost. Many people simply aren't planning a move.

## Give the relationship a human owner

Automation can remember a home anniversary, schedule an approved update, and flag an unanswered reply.

A person should handle dissatisfaction, sensitive circumstances, pricing discussions, and decisions about a future move.

Create a backup for the owner too. If the client replies while that agent is away, someone needs to acknowledge the message and arrange follow-up.

Review a small sample of scheduled messages each month. Check names, dates, brokerage details, relevance, and whether the client already replied elsewhere.

Automation where it makes sense, humans where they matter.

## Start with the clients you promised to contact

Before creating another campaign, find the promises already sitting in your notes.

“Send me an update this fall.” “Check back after our lease decision.” “Let us know how to plan the next move.”

Complete those first. Then build a repeatable calendar around the service people actually want.
