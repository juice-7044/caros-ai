# Insurance Jobs Don't Stall. They're Abandoned. Here's the Follow-Up System.

An inspection is complete. The estimate is ready. The customer is waiting for an update.

Your board says “pending insurance.”

It said that last Tuesday too.

The title names a preventable process problem. Some insurance-related jobs face legitimate delays or never proceed. An insurer's review is not something your roofing company controls.

What you can control is whether someone knows the current status, owns the next task, and keeps the customer informed.

Roofing insurance job follow-up should make that work visible. It should not become a machine for sending “just checking in” until everyone stops reading.

None of these are failures of good roofers. They're failures of process.

## Replace the parking space with a real status

“Pending insurance” can describe several different situations.

The homeowner may be deciding whether to contact their insurer. They may be waiting for a response. Your team may owe inspection documents. Someone may have requested additional information.

Those situations need different actions.

Create statuses your team can verify: customer considering next steps, contractor documents needed, awaiting customer update, information requested, scope discussion needed, customer decision pending, or scheduling review.

Add the date and source of the update. A customer's statement is different from a document your team received.

Don't advance a job because an automated message was delivered. Delivery confirms that a message traveled. It doesn't confirm approval, agreement, or readiness for production.

## Make ownership smaller than the whole job

A salesperson can own the relationship without completing every task.

For each open file, record the next task, task owner, due date, and dependency. Keep a separate customer-facing contact responsible for explaining progress.

For example: “Office coordinator uploads our inspection photos by Thursday. Salesperson calls the homeowner Friday to confirm receipt and discuss questions.”

That is actionable. “Chase insurance” is not.

If a task depends on another party, record when your team will check again. Don't invent a completion date for work outside your control.

When the owner is absent, the backup should see the same notes. A customer should not need to retell the entire story because someone took a day off.

## Ask for one specific thing at a time

Document follow-up works better when the recipient knows exactly what to do.

“Send the paperwork” leaves too much interpretation. Name the document and explain the contractor task it supports.

Try this email:

“Hi [Name], to complete [specific contractor task], we still need [document]. Please upload it through [approved secure method]. If you don't have it, reply and we'll clarify the next step.”

Give the request an owner and a date for review. If the document arrives, stop the reminder and move the task forward.

Store files in the approved customer record with appropriate access. Don't paste sensitive claim details into broad team chats or an automated marketing sequence.

The goal is less searching and fewer repeated requests. Customers should not have to send the same file to three people.

## Build a cadence around the next decision

Start with the homeowner's situation and preferred contact method. Agree when the next update makes sense.

A suggested operating cadence might include an estimate-receipt check, a scheduled document check, and a decision conversation. Treat these as starting points, not universal deadlines.

After each substantive update, reset the next action. If the customer says a decision is expected next month, don't send tomorrow's generic nudge.

Use a message such as: “Our last note says [verified status]. Has anything changed? We can help locate our inspection documents or explain our estimate.”

When there is no new information, say so clearly. Confirm the next agreed check-in rather than pretending progress happened.

Useful follow-up reduces uncertainty. Repeated follow-up without a purpose adds noise.

## Automate reminders; keep judgment with people

Automation can create a task when a next-action date arrives. It can remind a coordinator to check whether a requested file exists.

It can also send an approved acknowledgment and pause when a reply arrives.

It should not decide whether damage is covered, interpret policy language, promise payment, or infer that a claim was approved.

Route coverage questions, disputed amounts, complaints, and technical scope questions to the appropriate qualified person. Keep contractor communications within your team's authorized role.

For customer messages, use your approved permission, sending-hours, and opt-out practices. A stop request must end the applicable automated outreach.

Automation where it makes sense, humans where they matter. In this workflow, judgment matters most when the facts are incomplete.

## Put a dollar value on the follow-up gap carefully

Consider an illustrative example, not an industry average or CAROS performance claim.

Suppose ten estimates have no scheduled next conversation. Assume 20% could eventually become signed jobs after appropriate follow-up. Assume average contract value is $15,000.

10 × 20% × $15,000 = **$30,000 in modeled contract revenue opportunity**.

This is neither expected insurer payment nor guaranteed revenue. It is not profit. A contract may change, be canceled, or never be signed.

Use your own historical conversion rate and job values. Exclude files already closed or awaiting a genuine external milestone.

The [CAROS Revenue Leakage Calculator](https://revenue-leakage-calculator.getcaros.com/) can help frame the broader opportunity. The file review tells you which actions are actually available today.

Revenue first. But honest inputs first, too.

## Review aging by reason, not just days

A thirty-day-old file with a documented next event is different from a ten-day-old file nobody owns.

Review both age and reason. Ask what is missing, who can supply it, and what your team has agreed to do next.

Track overdue internal tasks separately from external waiting periods. Otherwise, your team can appear late for work it cannot control—or hide internal delays behind an insurer label.

Useful measures include open files without an owner, files without a dated next action, overdue contractor tasks, and estimates awaiting a customer decision.

Record why work closes: customer declined, chose another contractor, no work recommended, timing changed, or another known reason.

Don't manufacture a reason just to clean up the board. Unknown is a valid starting point for improving your records.

## Close the loop when the answer is no

Persistent follow-up should still respect a decision.

When a homeowner declines, record the outcome and stop the active sequence. If they ask for contact later, record the requested timing and appropriate permission.

If there is no response after your agreed cadence, send a courteous closing message. Explain how to reopen the conversation.

Then close the active task rather than leaving it overdue forever.

A clean pipeline helps the team focus. It also prevents inflated forecasts built from jobs that no longer have a next step.

[CAROS for roofing companies](https://getcaros.com/en/industries/roofing) connects claim-status tracking with inspection booking and estimate follow-up. Use that process to make the next action visible, not to pretend every delay can be automated away.

Open five “pending” files today. If each one leaves the review with a verified status, an owner, and a next action, you've built something useful.
