# Your Agreements Aren't Churning. They're Just Never Renewed.

Sixty agreements expire next month. The office has a list. Nobody owns the next action.

Some customers will leave deliberately. Others might stay if someone explains the renewal and asks.

Both appear as lost agreements on a dashboard. They need different responses.

The headline is a challenge to inspect your process, not a claim that every cancellation is preventable. Price, service quality, relocation, and changing needs all matter.

But before you call the problem churn, check whether an accurate renewal invitation ever reached the customer.

None of these are failures of good HVAC pros. They're failures of process.

## Separate a decision to leave from a missing invitation

Pull the last completed expiration cohort. That means agreements due to expire during the same defined period.

Sort the unrenewed accounts into useful categories: explicit decline, no response after verified outreach, no outreach recorded, payment issue, service issue, and incorrect record.

Don't guess at reasons. “No response” doesn't mean “too expensive.” A failed email doesn't mean the customer rejected your company.

Look for manual and automatic renewals separately. Their workflows and reporting need different checks. An existing automatic-renewal customer shouldn't receive a confusing request to buy the same coverage again.

This first pass tells you whether the next fix belongs in communication, billing, service fulfillment, or the offer itself.

## Count the revenue without inflating it

Imagine 100 eligible agreements reach expiration. Sixty renew. Your cohort renewal rate is 60%.

Now model another 10 renewals at an illustrative $240 annual agreement price.

**10 × $240 = $2,400 in additional annual agreement sales.**

That's hypothetical scenario math. It isn't a benchmark, a promised improvement, or evidence that reminders caused those renewals.

It also isn't profit. Your company must fulfill the included services and carry the associated costs. Payment timing may differ from when revenue is recognized.

Don't add imagined replacement sales to make the number look bigger. First measure the agreement business on its own.

## Start renewal work before the expiration date

A reasonable starting process for manual renewals opens 30 days before expiration. The correct timing depends on your terms and customer expectations.

The first message should state the verified end date, renewal price, term, and benefits. Include a secure way to review the agreement or speak with someone.

If there is no response, a later reminder can ask whether the customer has questions. A call task near expiration gives a person a chance to resolve uncertainty.

At every step, recheck the account. A customer who renewed yesterday shouldn't receive today's warning about losing coverage.

Keep the sequence short enough to supervise. More touches don't automatically create a better customer experience.

## Sell the agreement you actually provide

“Keep your benefits” is vague. Name the benefits that apply to that customer's plan.

That might include specific maintenance visits, approved pricing terms, or a defined scheduling benefit. Use the agreement's actual language when describing limits and exclusions.

Avoid turning a maintenance plan into a guarantee that equipment won't fail. Don't imply a homeowner needs replacement equipment because they haven't renewed.

Review whether the prior term was fulfilled. If a visit remains outstanding, address it before sending another purchase request.

A good renewal conversation connects the customer's needs with a clear offer. It gives them enough information to make a decision without having to decode your office shorthand.

## Give every reply a human owner

A homeowner replies, “We paid last week.” Another says, “Your technician never came back.” A third asks whether both systems are covered.

Those replies need different actions. A generic reminder won't resolve any of them.

Pause automated outreach when a reply arrives. Assign the conversation to a named person with access to the account.

Use simple routing: payment questions to the billing owner, service problems to the service manager, and coverage questions to someone authorized to explain terms.

Record the outcome and next action. When the homeowner asks for a call next month, schedule that task and stop the current cadence.

Automation where it makes sense, humans where they matter.

## Write messages that make the answer easy

Try this opening for a manual renewal:

“Hi [First name], your [Company] maintenance agreement ends [date]. Renewal is [approved price] for [term], including [verified benefits]. Would you like the details, help with a question, or a call?”

Each field must come from a verified record. Send through a permitted channel and respect contact preferences.

The message gives the customer a useful choice. It doesn't assume they've already decided.

For a price objection, ask what feels hard to justify. Explain approved options. Don't invent a saving or discount to rescue the conversation.

For a service complaint, stop selling and investigate. A renewal workflow should expose problems your team needs to solve.

## Build a stop button into the process

Before launch, define what stops further reminders: confirmed renewal, explicit decline, opt-out, wrong contact, unresolved complaint, or manual hold.

Payment confirmation needs a reliable record update. A clicked link alone isn't proof of a completed renewal.

Check synchronization between your agreement, billing, and communication systems. If one updates later than another, decide how reminders remain paused while records reconcile.

Test duplicate accounts. Two records for one agreement can produce two invitations and an awkward conversation about double payment.

Ask someone outside the setup process to test it. They may notice the confusing message everyone else has read twenty times.

## Read the scorecard by expiration cohort

Measure eligible agreements, verified invitations, replies, completed renewals, declines, and unresolved accounts. Keep delivery failures separate from customer silence.

Report renewal rate against the same cohort denominator. Mixing last month's expirations with this month's new enrollments can hide the actual problem.

Record revenue and cash collection separately where needed. Track service obligations alongside agreement sales so growth doesn't obscure fulfillment work.

Compare similar groups over time. Price changes, customer mix, and seasonality can change the result even when the workflow stays the same.

The scorecard should tell your team what to do next. If delivery failures rise, fix the list. If complaints rise, inspect the service experience.

## Make one small cohort work before expanding

Start with a batch the office can review individually. Confirm every date, price, benefit, and open service issue.

Run the sequence. Review the replies together. Fix incorrect assumptions before adding more customers.

Your database shouldn't be a digital graveyard. An expiration date should create a clear task, rather than wait for someone to remember it.
