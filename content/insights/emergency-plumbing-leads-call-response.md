# When a Pipe Bursts, They Call Whoever Picks Up First

It is 6:17 p.m. Your technician is under a sink. The office has closed. A new caller hears voicemail.

Across town, that caller is looking at water spreading across the floor. They need to know who can help.

They may leave a message. They may also call the next plumber before your technician reaches the truck.

You paid for that lead. A missed ring deserves more than a hopeful callback tomorrow.

None of these are failures of skilled plumbers. They're failures of process.

The fix starts with a practical question: what happens between an unanswered call and a human taking responsibility?

## Your response has two clocks

The first clock measures acknowledgement. Did the caller receive any useful sign that their request reached you?

The second measures human response. Did someone review the problem, check service availability, and explain the next step?

An automatic text can address the first clock. It cannot, by itself, settle the second.

That distinction matters during urgent plumbing requests. “We received your call” does not mean “a technician is on the way.”

Make both timestamps visible. Otherwise, a fast acknowledgement can hide an unanswered conversation sitting in an inbox all evening.

Choose response windows your team can actually meet. A solo operator and a business with staffed overnight coverage need different promises.

## Review the calls before buying more leads

Pull a complete month's call log. Mark unanswered calls during the hours you want to examine.

Then clean the list. Remove spam, supplier calls, duplicate attempts, and existing appointment updates. Separate requests outside your service area.

One homeowner calling three times is one opportunity. Counting rings as leads makes the problem look larger than it is.

For each eligible request, find the eventual outcome. Was it booked, completed, declined, lost, or still open?

Also record whether your existing callback process recovered it. Those jobs already belong in your baseline.

This creates a useful starting point: unique eligible opportunities that lacked a live answer and remain unresolved or lost.

You can improve that process without pretending every missed caller would have become a customer.

## Put dollars around a scenario, not a promise

Consider a hypothetical plumbing business with 20 eligible unanswered after-hours opportunities in a month.

Its existing callbacks already recovered five. That leaves 15 unresolved or lost opportunities.

Assume an improved response process could turn 20% of those 15 into additional completed jobs. Assume average collected revenue of $450.

The calculation is:

**15 opportunities × 20% assumed completion rate × $450 = $1,350 modeled monthly revenue.**

These are example inputs, not industry averages or CAROS results. Replace all three with figures suited to your business.

Revenue also has costs attached. At an assumed 40% contribution margin, that scenario creates $540 before added response costs.

Subtract a hypothetical $250 monthly response cost and the modeled contribution becomes $290.

That $250 is an example expense, not CAROS pricing. Include your own software, messaging, answering, and callback labor costs.

Revenue first. Then check whether the extra work is worth doing.

## Build the handoff around actual coverage

Write down your staffed hours, on-call coverage, service area, and capacity. Then build your workflow around those facts.

When a call goes unanswered, send an appropriate acknowledgement and create a task for a named person. Give that task a deadline.

During staffed coverage, alert the person who can review the request. If they miss the deadline, route it to a backup.

Outside staffed coverage, state when a person will respond. Do not imply that an unattended inbox is emergency dispatch.

A useful after-hours message might say:

“[Business]: Our office is closed. We return calls starting [day/time]. This inbox is not monitored for emergencies. If you need immediate service, contact an available provider. STOP to opt out.”

It is an honest handoff. It lets the caller make an informed decision.

## Give people the decisions that require judgment

Your team should decide whether the request fits your service area, whether the schedule has room, and what happens next.

Automation can collect a ZIP code and brief description. It can organize a callback queue and surface overdue requests.

It should not invent availability, diagnose a hazardous situation, or promise an arrival time without confirmation.

For immediate danger, direct callers toward appropriate emergency services. Do not turn a lead-capture workflow into an automated safety adviser.

When you do offer booking, make clear whether the customer is requesting a time or confirming an available appointment.

Automation where it makes sense, humans where they matter.

## Keep the marketing source attached

A recovered missed call might have started with a paid search ad, a referral, or a returning customer.

Keep that original source on the record. Add the response workflow as an assisted touch.

Otherwise, your report may credit every recovered job to “automation” and erase the marketing that generated the request.

Track the customer journey through completed work and collected revenue. A reply is useful. A booked visit is progress. Neither is a paid invoice.

Use consistent definitions across your office and field teams. If one person marks “booked” at first contact, your comparisons will fall apart.

## Test the awkward cases before the weekend

Call your own number while the team is busy. Then test after closing.

Try a repeated call, an opted-out contact, a withheld number, and a reply when the assigned person is unavailable.

Check whether the text arrives, the task appears, the backup receives the alert, and the sequence stops after a reply.

Also check what happens when messages cannot be delivered. Your process needs a visible exception, not a quiet assumption that texting worked.

Before activating messages, confirm consent, sender registration, and opt-out handling with your provider for your setup. Keep service replies separate from promotional campaigns.

## Give the team one short weekly review

Review eligible unanswered opportunities, delivered acknowledgements, first human response time, bookings, completed jobs, and collected revenue.

Look at complaints and missed callback promises, too. More messages do not automatically mean better service.

Compare the same types of requests across similar periods. Weather, staffing, service mix, and advertising can all change the numbers.

If the workflow produces replies but few completed jobs, read the conversations. The problem may be coverage, capacity, pricing, or an unclear next step.

Improve the weakest handoff before adding another reminder.
