# The 18-Month Buyer: Nurturing Leads Who Aren't Ready Yet

“We love the area. We're probably eighteen months away.”

You hear it between showings. You make a mental note. You promise to stay in touch.

Then Friday becomes next month.

The buyer hasn't rejected you. Their calendar simply runs on a different clock than your commission pipeline.

Real estate lead nurture gives that long decision a useful structure. It keeps the next conversation from depending on memory.

None of these are failures of good real estate pros. They're failures of process.

Your database shouldn't be a digital graveyard. But sending eighteen months of “Just checking in” isn't much of an improvement.

## Replace “not ready” with a specific reason

“Not ready” is a status label. It doesn't tell you how to help.

The buyer might be waiting for a lease to end. They might want to build savings, confirm a relocation, or understand the buying process.

Ask a small question: “What needs to happen before moving becomes realistic?”

Then ask what support would be useful while they wait.

Record the expected timing, the reason behind it, the preferred contact channel, and an agreed next conversation date.

Keep the record practical. You don't need a detailed account of private family or financial circumstances to remember “reconnect before lease renewal.”

Avoid turning a timing conversation into a qualification interrogation. The purpose is a useful plan, not pressure.

## Build nurture around decisions, not arbitrary dates

A monthly message can be reasonable when the buyer wants one. It becomes noise when timing ignores their situation.

Use a simple set of stages:

- **Planning:** the buyer wants education and occasional updates.
- **Preparing:** a specific milestone is approaching.
- **Active:** the buyer wants a current search or consultation.
- **Paused:** the buyer has asked for less contact or no contact.

These stages describe readiness. They aren't predictions that a transaction will close.

A planning-stage contact might receive an agreed monthly note. A preparing-stage contact might prefer a scheduled conversation before lease renewal.

An active buyer needs an agent-led plan. A paused contact needs the pause respected.

Make stage changes explicit. A webpage visit alone shouldn't trigger a sudden burst of calls.

## Write a sequence a real person would want

The best nurture message has a reason to exist beyond keeping your name visible.

Start with a welcome note that reflects the conversation.

“You mentioned moving after your lease ends next summer. I'll send the planning checklist we discussed and reconnect in [month]. Would a monthly email be useful until then?”

Then deliver what you promised.

For a first-time buyer, that could be an overview of the consultation process. For a relocating buyer, it could be a neutral list of practical research questions.

Use current, verified information. When a question requires lending, legal, tax, or other specialist guidance, arrange the appropriate introduction.

Don't let a general sequence pretend to know someone's financing options or future market conditions.

A useful check-in asks one answerable question:

“Is your target month still [month], or should I move our next conversation?”

That reply improves the plan. “Any updates?” mostly transfers the work to the buyer.

## An eighteen-month framework you can adapt

The timeline below is an operating example, not a required contact schedule.

**At the first conversation:** record timing, useful topics, permission, preferred channel, and the next review date. Send the promised resource.

**During the planning period:** use the agreed cadence. Share relevant education or a concise, sourced market update. Offer an easy way to reduce frequency or pause.

**Before an agreed milestone:** create a task for the agent. Ask whether circumstances changed and whether a planning consultation would help.

**When the buyer becomes active:** stop the general nurture sequence. Move into a personal search or consultation workflow.

**When timing moves back:** update the plan without making the buyer defend the delay.

**When the buyer declines or opts out:** stop the relevant outreach and preserve the suppression record.

One contact may move through this in three months. Another may stay in planning for two years. The workflow should follow their decision.

## Automate the reminders. Keep the relationship human.

Automation can remember dates, deliver approved resources, and create tasks. It can help an agent see who's due for attention.

That is valuable repeat work.

It shouldn't manufacture a personal memory or imply that the agent personally wrote a message they never reviewed.

Keep one named owner on the record. Give replies somewhere monitored to land. Pause scheduled messages when a conversation starts.

If an agent leaves the team, reassign contacts and review the promised follow-up dates. A working sequence with an abandoned inbox is still an abandoned relationship.

Automation where it makes sense, humans where they matter.

The buyer gets continuity. Your team gets fewer reminders living in someone's head.

## Show the dollar math without inventing lifetime value

Suppose a team paid $4,000 to acquire 100 unique leads. That's **$40 per lead**.

In this hypothetical cohort, 30 people ask for a later conversation. Their share of acquisition spending is 30 × $40 = **$1,200**.

That spending has already happened. It doesn't mean the team owns $1,200 of future revenue. It means these contacts deserve a deliberate decision about appropriate follow-up.

Now model a scenario rather than presenting a forecast.

Assume two of those contacts eventually close, with $4,000 retained commission per transaction. That's **$8,000 before nurture and other operating costs**.

If the nurture program costs $1,200 over the period, subtract it. Also account for agent time, service costs, and any other costs relevant to your business.

The two closings are assumptions, not promised results. The point is to make the economics visible enough to test.

## Measure movement, not message volume

“We sent 3,000 emails” describes activity. It doesn't describe buyer progress.

Track how many contacts have a next step, how many reply, how many move into an active conversation, and how many attend consultations.

Keep opt-outs and negative replies visible. They tell you whether relevance or frequency needs attention.

Connect eventual signed clients and closings to the original source and arrival cohort. Preserve later nurture touches as assists rather than overwriting the acquisition source.

Long decisions require patient reporting. Don't label a planning contact “lost” because they didn't transact inside a monthly dashboard window.

Don't count every later closing as caused by nurture either. The buyer's circumstances, agent relationship, and market conditions all matter.

## Give ten contacts a better next step

Start small. Choose ten contacts marked “later” whose permissions and preferences support follow-up.

Read their notes. Remove anyone already represented elsewhere, suppressed, or already in an active workflow. Confirm ownership before sending anything.

Ask whether the original timing still fits. Offer a useful resource or a dated conversation. Record the answer.
