# From Drain Clear to Sewer Line: Automating the Upsell You Keep Forgetting

The drain is flowing. The invoice is paid. Your technician has notes about a recommended next step.

Those notes stay in the job record. The homeowner hears nothing. Three months later, someone wonders whether anyone followed up.

Your database shouldn't be a digital graveyard.

But a completed drain clearing is not automatic evidence that a customer needs sewer work. The follow-up must start with documented findings.

None of these are failures of skilled plumbers. They're failures of process.

The useful automation is a bridge between a technician's recommendation and a homeowner's decision. It gives the right person the right next step.

Revenue first. Trust is part of the process that earns it.

## Start with the recommendation, not the campaign

Before building a sequence, decide what qualifies a customer for it.

A technician may recommend a further assessment based on observed conditions. Another job may be complete with no additional work recommended.

Those customers should not enter the same follow-up sequence.

Record the finding, what remains unknown, the recommended next step, and whether the homeowner wants further contact.

If an inspection is needed before a repair recommendation, say that. Do not turn an open question into an automated statement that replacement is necessary.

Your workflow should reflect what a qualified person documented. A software tag cannot diagnose a sewer line.

## Capture the information while the job is fresh

Give technicians a short, consistent closeout form. It should help the office understand the conversation without reconstructing it from scattered notes.

Include the completed service, relevant observations, recommended action, urgency as assessed by the technician, and the customer's response.

Record the approved contact channel and any agreed follow-up date. Attach the estimate or report when one exists.

Also record “no additional work recommended.” That field prevents a generic upsell from overruling the actual service outcome.

Keep safety-related recommendations in a separate human-managed path. Urgent concerns should not wait behind a routine marketing reminder.

The form is complete when another team member can explain the next step accurately.

## Separate the three conversations

One customer needs an assessment to understand the problem. Another has an estimate and needs answers. A third wants no further contact.

Build different paths for each.

For an assessment, explain why it was recommended and what it can clarify. State any approved charges before booking.

For an estimate, make the scope and options easy to review. Invite questions about timing, access, and what the work includes.

For a declined recommendation, record the decision and close the sequence. If the homeowner requests a later reminder, use that date.

Do not keep nudging every customer because the system can. A respectful stop is part of a functioning follow-up process.

## Write the first message from the job notes

Here is a template for a documented, non-urgent recommendation:

“[Business]: Following your drain service on [date], [technician] recommended [documented next step] because [observed finding]. Would you like a call to discuss it? Reply STOP to opt out.”

Every field needs a real entry. If the finding is missing, send the record back for human review.

For an estimate already provided, use a different message:

“[Business]: Any questions about the [scope] estimate we sent on [date]? We can explain the options or close the follow-up if your plans changed. STOP to opt out.”

The goal is a useful conversation. Avoid invented urgency, assumed diagnoses, and discounts nobody approved.

## Use a short sequence with real exits

An example sequence might start with a service summary after job closeout. Include a recommendation only when the technician documented one.

For customers who agreed to further contact, create a follow-up task a few business days later. Let them choose a different date.

If the first check-in gets no answer, send one final close-the-loop message at an appropriate interval. Then close routine outreach.

This is a workflow example, not a proven optimal cadence. Adjust it to the recommendation, customer preference, and staffed capacity.

Stop automated reminders on reply, booking, decline, or opt-out. Route the conversation to the person who can answer it.

Before launch, confirm messaging permission, registration, and opt-out handling with your provider. A service record alone is not blanket permission for promotion.

## Show the revenue math and the limits

Suppose a hypothetical month includes 30 drain customers with documented recommendations and appropriate permission for follow-up.

Assume 20% book an assessment. That produces six assessments.

Assume half result in accepted, completed additional work. That produces three jobs.

At an assumed $2,000 average collected revenue per additional job:

**30 × 20% × 50% × $2,000 = $6,000 modeled additional-work revenue.**

These are illustrative inputs, not plumbing averages or CAROS results. Your findings, service mix, and economics may be very different.

At an assumed 35% contribution margin, $6,000 produces $2,100 before added assessment, outreach, and workflow costs not already included.

Subtract a hypothetical $600 in those remaining costs and modeled contribution becomes $1,500.

Avoid counting assessment costs twice. Also avoid counting the same homeowner twice when estimating repeat-customer value.

## Measure appropriateness as well as revenue

Track the number of eligible recommendations, customers contacted, replies, assessments booked, estimates accepted, and additional jobs completed.

Connect collected revenue to the original customer and job record. Keep follow-up activity visible as an assisted touch.

Assisted revenue does not prove the sequence caused every job. Some homeowners would have returned without a reminder.

Review declines, complaints, and incorrect recommendations alongside sales. Those signals can expose a bad enrollment rule quickly.

If an assessment finds no additional work is needed, record that outcome. The workflow did its job by getting a clear answer.

Do not evaluate technicians solely on how much extra work follows a drain visit. That creates the wrong incentive.

## Make service agreements their own decision

A homeowner considering further assessment should not automatically receive a service-agreement pitch in the same thread.

Finish the current conversation first. Offer an agreement when it fits the customer's needs and your actual service terms.

Explain the price, included services, exclusions, renewal terms, and how to ask questions. Do not invent savings or imply universal suitability.

Track agreement interest separately from sewer-work recommendations. Otherwise, your office cannot tell which conversation is still open.

The same principle applies to water-heater and repipe estimates. Follow up on a documented recommendation with a clear owner and stopping point.

## Turn the record into a useful next step

Start with a small group of complete job records. Have a human review each enrollment before expanding the workflow.

Read the messages as a homeowner would. Can they understand why you contacted them and what decision they are being asked to make?
